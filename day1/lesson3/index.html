    Lesson 3 — Four numerical engineering exercises

    Format: Four short group calculations followed by a shared solution

    Required output: Completed calculation and interpretation sheet

    This lesson converts the visual observations from Lesson 2 into quantitative engineering reasoning. Work on each exercise for approximately six minutes, then use the collapsible solution during the four-minute class check.

    Learning objectives

    By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

    • solve the longitudinal force balance at equilibrium;
    • calculate raw and saturated P-controller commands;
    • extract common response metrics from stated measurements;
    • step through a discrete PI integrator;
    • explain the physical meaning of every result and unit.

    Working rules

    1. Write the equation symbolically before inserting numbers.
    2. Include units in every intermediate result.
    3. State whether the numerical answer is physically feasible.
    4. Reveal the worked solution only after the group attempt.

    Calculators and short Python expressions are allowed, but a numerical result without an equation and interpretation is incomplete.

    For each ten-minute exercise, use approximately six minutes for group work and four minutes to compare methods. If you finish early, attempt the engineering challenge before revealing the worked solution.

    Model, force definitions and sign convention

    All four exercises use the same one-dimensional longitudinal model. The positive direction is the vehicle's forward direction, up the road:

    \[ m a =F_\mathrm{drive} -F_\mathrm{rolling} -F_\mathrm{drag} -F_\mathrm{hill}. \]

    The individual forces are:

    Quantity Definition Physical meaning Unit
    \(F_\mathrm{drive}\) \(uF_{\max}\) Force generated by the powertrain at the road \(\mathrm{N}\)
    \(F_\mathrm{rolling}\) constant \(180\ \mathrm{N}\) in this simplified model Tyre and bearing losses opposing motion \(\mathrm{N}\)
    \(F_\mathrm{drag}\) \(c_\mathrm{drag}v^2\) for forward motion Quadratic speed-dependent resistance \(\mathrm{N}\)
    \(F_\mathrm{hill}\) \(mg\sin\theta\) Component of gravity parallel to an uphill road \(\mathrm{N}\)
    \(F_\mathrm{net}\) sum of all signed forces Force responsible for acceleration \(\mathrm{N}\)

    Here, \(u\) is a dimensionless normalized command. A value of \(u=0.35\) means that the requested drive force is \(35\%\) of \(F_{\max}\):

    \[ F_\mathrm{drive}=0.35(4500)=1575\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    The force unit newton is defined by Newton's second law:

    \[ 1\ \mathrm{N}=1\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}. \]

    What exactly is the unit of \(c_\mathrm{drag}\)?

    The quadratic drag model is:

    \[ F_\mathrm{drag}=c_\mathrm{drag}v^2. \]

    Therefore, the coefficient must convert squared speed into force:

    \[ [c_\mathrm{drag}] =\frac{\mathrm{N}}{(\mathrm{m/s})^2} =\frac{\mathrm{N\,s^2}}{\mathrm{m^2}} =\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}}. \]

    Thus, all of the following are equivalent units:

    \[ \boxed{ 4\ \mathrm{N/(m/s)^2} =4\ \mathrm{N\,s^2/m^2} =4\ \mathrm{kg/m} } \]

    For example, at \(v=15\ \mathrm{m/s}\):

    \[ F_\mathrm{drag} =\left(4\ \frac{\mathrm{N}}{(\mathrm{m/s})^2}\right) \left(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\right)^2 =900\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    The speed units cancel correctly, leaving newtons. Do not confuse the dimensional coefficient \(c_\mathrm{drag}\) with the dimensionless aerodynamic drag coefficient \(C_D\). If only aerodynamic drag is represented, then:

    \[ c_\mathrm{drag}=\frac{1}{2}\rho C_DA, \]

    where air density \(\rho\) has unit \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(C_D\) is dimensionless, and frontal area \(A\) has unit \(\mathrm{m^2}\). The resulting unit is again \(\mathrm{kg/m}\).

    Converting hill force to slope angle and road grade

    For a vehicle of mass \(m\) on a road inclined by angle \(\theta\):

    \[ F_\mathrm{hill}=mg\sin\theta. \]

    If the force is known, the equivalent angle is:

    \[ \theta=\arcsin\left(\frac{F_\mathrm{hill}}{mg}\right). \]

    Road grade is normally expressed as vertical rise divided by horizontal run:

    \[ \mathrm{grade}\,[\%]=100\tan\theta. \]

    Angle and grade are related but are not numerically identical. For the \(1200\ \mathrm{kg}\) vehicle used below, with \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\):

    Opposing hill force Equivalent angle Approximate road grade
    \(0\ \mathrm{N}\) \(0^\circ\) \(0\%\)
    \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\) \(4.87^\circ\) \(8.52\%\)
    \(1500\ \mathrm{N}\) \(7.32^\circ\) \(12.85\%\)
    \(4000\ \mathrm{N}\) \(19.86^\circ\) \(36.13\%\)

    In Exercise 1, “a \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\) hill” therefore means that the model adds a constant \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\) force opposing forward motion. For a \(1200\ \mathrm{kg}\) vehicle, this is equivalent to driving uphill at approximately \(4.87^\circ\). On a downhill road, \(F_\mathrm{hill}\) would have the opposite sign and gravity would assist the vehicle.

    A useful modelling distinction

    If \(F_\mathrm{hill}\) is supplied directly as a fixed force, it remains the same when mass is changed. If the physical road angle \(\theta\) is fixed, then \(F_\mathrm{hill}=mg\sin\theta\) increases with mass. Always state which quantity is being held constant.

    Exercise 1 — Open-loop equilibrium speed

    Given

    \[ F_{\max}=4500\ \mathrm{N}, \quad F_\mathrm{rolling}=180\ \mathrm{N}, \quad c_\mathrm{drag}=4\ \mathrm{N/(m/s)^2}=4\ \mathrm{kg/m}, \quad u=0.35, \quad m=1200\ \mathrm{kg}. \]

    At equilibrium:

    \[ uF_{\max} -F_\mathrm{rolling} -c_\mathrm{drag}v^2 -F_\mathrm{hill}=0. \]

    Tasks

    1. Draw or list the four longitudinal forces and mark their directions.
    2. Calculate the drive force produced by \(u=0.35\).
    3. Calculate the equilibrium speed on a flat road.
    4. Convert a \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\) opposing hill force into an equivalent road angle for the \(1200\ \mathrm{kg}\) vehicle.
    5. Calculate the equilibrium speed with that uphill disturbance.
    6. Calculate the command required to maintain \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\) on that hill.
    7. State whether the required command is feasible and calculate the remaining drive-force margin.
    8. Explain why vehicle mass is absent when the hill is given directly as a force, but reappears when the hill is specified as a road angle.

    Recording area

    Result Symbolic expression Numerical value Feasible?
    Drive force at \(u=0.35\)
    Equivalent slope angle and grade
    Flat-road equilibrium speed
    Hill equilibrium speed
    Command at \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
    Worked solution

    Step 1 — Define the drive force.

    \[ F_\mathrm{drive}=uF_{\max} =0.35(4500\ \mathrm{N}) =1575\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    On a flat road, this forward force is opposed by rolling resistance and quadratic drag.

    Step 2 — Calculate the flat-road equilibrium speed.

    At equilibrium, acceleration and net force are zero:

    \[ 0=F_\mathrm{drive}-F_\mathrm{rolling}-c_\mathrm{drag}v^2. \]

    Rearranging:

    \[ v_\mathrm{flat} =\sqrt{ \frac{uF_{\max}-F_\mathrm{rolling}} {c_\mathrm{drag}} } =\sqrt{ \frac{0.35(4500)-180}{4} } =18.67\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    The unit check is:

    \[ \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{N/(m/s)^2}}} =\sqrt{(\mathrm{m/s})^2} =\mathrm{m/s}. \]

    Step 3 — Interpret the \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\) hill.

    The equivalent angle is:

    \[ \theta =\arcsin\left( \frac{1000}{1200(9.81)} \right) =4.87^\circ. \]

    Step 4 — Calculate hill equilibrium speed.

    \[ v_\mathrm{hill} =\sqrt{ \frac{0.35(4500)-180-1000}{4} } =9.94\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    A real-valued equilibrium exists because the remaining force available for drag is positive:

    \[ 1575-180-1000=395\ \mathrm{N}>0. \]

    Step 5 — Find the command needed at \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\).

    First calculate the required resistance balance:

    \[ F_\mathrm{required} =180+4(15)^2+1000 =2080\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    Therefore:

    \[ u_\mathrm{eq} =\frac{F_\mathrm{required}}{F_{\max}} =\frac{2080}{4500} =0.462. \]

    The command is feasible because \(0.462<1\). At this operating point, the unused drive-force margin is:

    \[ F_\mathrm{margin}=4500-2080=2420\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    Mass affects transient acceleration through \(ma\). If the hill is already supplied as the fixed force \(1000\ \mathrm{N}\), then at equilibrium \(a=0\) and the inertial term vanishes. However, if the road is specified by its fixed angle, mass determines \(F_\mathrm{hill}=mg\sin\theta\) and therefore does affect equilibrium.

    Interpretation question

    The command was calibrated to \(u=0.35\) on a flat road. Why is increasing mass alone not enough to change the theoretical final flat-road speed in this model, even though a heavier vehicle clearly responds differently during acceleration?

    The distinction is between steady-state balance and transient dynamics. This conclusion also relies on the simplified assumptions that \(F_\mathrm{rolling}\) and \(F_{\max}\) do not change with mass. In a more realistic model, rolling resistance is often approximated by \(C_\mathrm{rr}mg\), so additional mass can also change the steady-state balance.

    Engineering challenge

    Calculate the minimum drive-force limit needed to maintain \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\) under a \(4000\ \mathrm{N}\) hill load. For a \(1200\ \mathrm{kg}\) vehicle, also calculate the equivalent road angle. Compare the required drive force with the default \(4500\ \mathrm{N}\) limit.

    Challenge answer
    \[ F_{\max,\mathrm{required}} =180+4(15)^2+4000 =5080\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    The default actuator is insufficient. Controller tuning cannot overcome the missing \(580\ \mathrm{N}\) of physical authority.

    The equivalent slope is:

    \[ \theta =\arcsin\left(\frac{4000}{1200(9.81)}\right) =19.86^\circ, \]

    This is an extremely steep road. The large value is useful as an actuator authority test, not as a typical passenger-car road.

    Exercise 2 — One P-control update

    Given

    \[ v_\mathrm{target}=15\ \mathrm{m/s}, \quad v=12\ \mathrm{m/s}, \quad K_P=0.35\,(\mathrm{m/s})^{-1}. \]

    The controller and actuator limits are:

    \[ e=v_\mathrm{target}-v, \qquad u_\mathrm{raw}=K_Pe, \qquad u=\operatorname{clip}(u_\mathrm{raw},-1,1). \]

    The vehicle parameters at this instant are:

    \[ m=1200\ \mathrm{kg}, \quad F_{\max}=4500\ \mathrm{N}, \quad F_\mathrm{rolling}=180\ \mathrm{N}, \quad c_\mathrm{drag}=4\ \mathrm{N/(m/s)^2}. \]

    Assume the actuator force has already reached the requested full-drive force and the road is flat.

    Because \(u\) is dimensionless while the speed error has unit \(\mathrm{m/s}\), \(K_P\) is not dimensionless in this formulation:

    \[ [K_P] =\frac{1}{\mathrm{m/s}} =\mathrm{s/m}. \]

    In informal controller-tuning discussions the unit is often omitted, but including it makes the equation dimensionally consistent.

    Tasks

    1. Calculate the speed error.
    2. Calculate the raw controller command.
    3. Apply the actuator saturation.
    4. Calculate aerodynamic drag at \(12\ \mathrm{m/s}\).
    5. Define and calculate drive force, net force and acceleration.
    6. Decide whether increasing \(K_P\) to \(0.70\) changes the physical acceleration at this exact instant.
    7. Repeat the force and acceleration calculation for a \(5^\circ\) uphill road. Calculate the corresponding hill force and road grade first.

    Recording area

    Quantity Equation Result
    Error \(e\)
    Raw command \(u_\mathrm{raw}\)
    Applied command \(u\)
    Drag force
    Drive force
    Hill force at \(5^\circ\)
    Net force
    Acceleration
    Worked solution
    \[ e=15-12=3\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]
    \[ u_\mathrm{raw}=0.35(3)=1.05. \]
    \[ u=\operatorname{clip}(1.05,-1,1)=1. \]

    The drag force is:

    \[ F_\mathrm{drag}=4(12)^2=576\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    The net force and acceleration are:

    \[ F_\mathrm{drive}=uF_{\max}=1(4500)=4500\ \mathrm{N}, \]
    \[ F_\mathrm{net} =F_\mathrm{drive}-F_\mathrm{rolling}-F_\mathrm{drag} =4500-180-576 =3744\ \mathrm{N}, \]
    \[ a=\frac{3744}{1200}=3.12\ \mathrm{m/s^2}. \]

    With \(K_P=0.70\), the raw command would be \(2.10\), but the applied command would still be \(1\). Under the stated assumption that actuator force has settled, the acceleration would therefore be unchanged at that instant.

    For the \(5^\circ\) uphill extension:

    \[ F_\mathrm{hill} =mg\sin\theta =1200(9.81)\sin(5^\circ) =1026\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    The road grade is:

    \[ 100\tan(5^\circ)=8.75\%. \]

    The net force and acceleration become:

    \[ F_\mathrm{net,hill} =4500-180-576-1026 =2718\ \mathrm{N}, \]
    \[ a_\mathrm{hill} =\frac{2718}{1200} =2.27\ \mathrm{m/s^2}. \]

    The same saturated command therefore produces less acceleration uphill because part of the available drive force must balance gravity.

    Interpretation question

    Why may the actual simulator acceleration still lag behind \(3.12\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) immediately after the command reaches \(u=1\)?

    The model includes a \(0.35\ \mathrm{s}\) actuator time constant. Command saturation is immediate, but physical actuator force approaches its requested value over time.

    Engineering challenge

    Use Euler integration with \(\Delta t=0.05\ \mathrm{s}\) to estimate the next speed if acceleration remained constant during one step:

    \[ v_{k+1}=v_k+a_k\Delta t. \]
    Challenge answer
    \[ v_{k+1}=12+3.12(0.05)=12.156\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    This is only a one-step approximation. Drag and controller error change as speed changes.

    Exercise 3 — Assessing a measured response

    Given

    A test from rest to \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\) produced:

    • 90% of target first reached at \(6.8\ \mathrm{s}\);
    • peak speed \(16.2\ \mathrm{m/s}\);
    • response permanently entered the \(\pm5\%\) band at \(9.4\ \mathrm{s}\);
    • mean speed over the final two seconds \(14.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\);
    • command saturated for \(18\%\) of samples;
    • RMSE \(3.2\ \mathrm{m/s}\).

    Interpret the reported measurements as follows:

    • rise time: the time required to first reach \(90\%\) of the target;
    • peak speed: the largest measured speed during the test;
    • settling time: the first time after which the speed never leaves the specified tolerance band;
    • final error: target speed minus the representative final measured speed;
    • saturation percentage: fraction of samples for which the applied command is at an actuator limit;
    • RMSE: the root mean square of the speed error over all \(N\) samples.

    For this test, the \(90\%\) threshold and the \(\pm5\%\) settling band are:

    \[ v_{90\%}=0.90(15)=13.5\ \mathrm{m/s}, \]
    \[ v_\mathrm{lower}=0.95(15)=14.25\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad v_\mathrm{upper}=1.05(15)=15.75\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    The RMSE definition is:

    \[ \mathrm{RMSE} =\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \left(v_{\mathrm{target},k}-v_k\right)^2}. \]

    It has the same unit as speed, \(\mathrm{m/s}\).

    Requirements:

    Metric Requirement
    Rise time \(<7.0\ \mathrm{s}\)
    Overshoot \(<10\%\)
    Settling time \(<10.0\ \mathrm{s}\)
    Absolute final error \(<0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
    Saturation \(<20\%\)

    Tasks

    1. State the rise time.
    2. Calculate the \(90\%\) threshold and verify what event defines the reported rise time.
    3. Calculate percentage overshoot.
    4. Calculate the numerical limits of the \(\pm5\%\) settling band.
    5. State the settling time.
    6. Calculate signed and absolute final error.
    7. Mark every requirement separately as pass or fail.
    8. Decide whether the controller passes the complete specification.
    9. Explain why RMSE cannot replace every other metric.

    Assessment table

    Metric Calculated value Requirement Pass/fail
    Rise time \(<7.0\ \mathrm{s}\)
    Overshoot \(<10\%\)
    Settling time \(<10.0\ \mathrm{s}\)
    Absolute final error \(<0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
    Saturation \(<20\%\)
    Worked solution

    The \(90\%\) speed threshold is:

    \[ 0.90(15)=13.5\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    The response first reaches this value at \(6.8\ \mathrm{s}\), so the rise time under the stated course definition is:

    \[ t_r=6.8\ \mathrm{s}. \]

    Overshoot is:

    \[ M_P= \frac{16.2-15}{15}\cdot100 =8\%. \]

    Settling time is:

    \[ t_s=9.4\ \mathrm{s}. \]

    The settling band is:

    \[ 15\pm0.05(15) =15\pm0.75\ \mathrm{m/s}, \]

    or \(14.25\) to \(15.75\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Reaching this band once is not sufficient; the response must remain inside it after \(t_s\).

    Signed final error is:

    \[ e_\mathrm{final}=15-14.6=0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}, \]

    so the absolute final error is also \(0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\).

    Metric Value Result
    Rise time \(6.8\ \mathrm{s}\) Pass
    Overshoot \(8\%\) Pass
    Settling time \(9.4\ \mathrm{s}\) Pass
    Absolute final error \(0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\) Fail
    Saturation \(18\%\) Pass

    The complete specification fails because every mandatory criterion must be satisfied. RMSE compresses error over the entire run into one number; it does not separately reveal overshoot, final offset or actuator usage.

    Discussion prompt

    Should the controller be rejected immediately, retuned, or accepted with a documented deviation? The numerical answer depends on whether the criteria are hard safety constraints, contractual acceptance requirements or tuning goals.

    Engineering extension — compare two incomplete summaries

    Controller A has lower RMSE but \(14\%\) overshoot. Controller B has higher RMSE but no overshoot and a small final error. Decide which controller you would choose for:

    1. an autonomous shuttle prioritizing passenger comfort;
    2. a test vehicle prioritizing rapid response;
    3. a safety-limited actuator that must never exceed the target.

    There is no universal answer without weighting the requirements. Connect the numerical metrics to the intended engineering application.

    Exercise 4 — PI accumulation and windup

    Given

    \[ K_P=0.30, \quad K_I=0.10, \quad \Delta t=0.5\ \mathrm{s}, \quad F_{\max}=4500\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    For three samples, the error remains:

    \[ e=4\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    Assume \(I_0=0\) and no anti-windup:

    \[ I_k=I_{k-1}+e_k\Delta t, \]
    \[ u_{\mathrm{raw},k}=K_Pe_k+K_II_k, \qquad u_k=\operatorname{clip}(u_{\mathrm{raw},k},-1,1). \]

    After sample 3, the error becomes \(-1\ \mathrm{m/s}\) for one sample.

    The units of the PI terms are worth checking. Error has unit \(\mathrm{m/s}\), while the accumulated integral state has unit:

    \[ [I]=[\text{speed error}][\text{time}] =\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{s} =\mathrm{m}. \]

    To produce a dimensionless command:

    \[ [K_P]=\mathrm{s/m}, \qquad [K_I]=\mathrm{m^{-1}}. \]

    Accordingly, interpret the numerical gains here as \(K_P=0.30\ \mathrm{s/m}\) and \(K_I=0.10\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

    Tasks

    1. Complete samples 1–3 in the table.
    2. Calculate the integral state after the error changes to \(-1\).
    3. Calculate the new raw and applied commands.
    4. Explain why the sign of the command is surprising.
    5. Convert the sample-4 command into requested drive force using \(F_\mathrm{drive}=uF_{\max}\).
    6. State what conditional anti-windup would do during samples 1–3.
    Sample Error \(e\) Integral \(I\) Raw command Applied command
    0 — 0.0 — —
    1 4.0
    2 4.0
    3 4.0
    4 -1.0
    Worked solution

    During the positive-error samples:

    Sample Error \(e\) Integral \(I\) Raw command Applied command
    1 4.0 2.0 1.40 1.00
    2 4.0 4.0 1.60 1.00
    3 4.0 6.0 1.80 1.00

    When the error becomes \(-1\ \mathrm{m/s}\):

    \[ I_4=6+(-1)(0.5)=5.5, \]
    \[ u_{\mathrm{raw},4} =0.30(-1)+0.10(5.5) =0.25. \]

    The applied command is \(u_4=0.25\). The vehicle is already too fast, but the stored positive integral still requests acceleration. Conditional anti-windup would pause integration while positive error pushes the already saturated command farther above \(1\).

    The corresponding requested drive force is:

    \[ F_\mathrm{drive} =u_4F_{\max} =0.25(4500) =1125\ \mathrm{N}. \]

    This does not necessarily mean the net force is \(1125\ \mathrm{N}\). Rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag and any hill force must still be subtracted. It also does not mean the physical actuator force changes instantaneously if actuator lag is included.

    Engineering challenge

    With \(I=5.5\), calculate how negative the error must become before the raw command reaches zero.

    Challenge answer

    Set:

    \[ 0=K_Pe+K_II. \]

    Therefore:

    \[ e=-\frac{K_II}{K_P} =-\frac{0.10(5.5)}{0.30} =-1.83\ \mathrm{m/s}. \]

    The vehicle must be approximately \(1.83\ \mathrm{m/s}\) too fast before the raw command stops requesting positive drive.

    Retrieval check

    Answer individually, then compare with a neighbour:

    1. At equilibrium, why is net force zero rather than actuator force zero?
    2. What is the difference between raw command and applied command?
    3. Can a controller pass four requirements and fail the overall specification?
    4. Why does PI control need an anti-windup mechanism?
    Answer check
    1. The non-zero actuator force balances resistance and disturbance forces.
    2. Raw command is the controller request; applied command is the clipped request that respects actuator limits.
    3. Yes. If all listed criteria are mandatory, one failure rejects the complete configuration.
    4. The integrator can otherwise accumulate error while the actuator cannot deliver the requested force, producing delayed recovery and overshoot.

    Reserve activity — Sensitivity to parameter error

    The estimated equilibrium speed uses:

    \[ v_\mathrm{eq} =\sqrt{ \frac{uF_{\max}-F_\mathrm{rolling}-F_\mathrm{hill}} {c_\mathrm{drag}} }. \]

    Change one parameter by \(+20\%\), recalculate the equilibrium speed, and rank which uncertainty matters most under the selected operating condition. Distinguish parameters that influence equilibrium from those, such as mass, that influence only the transient in this simplified model.
